A shared iPad flow for cross-border shipping.

Project overview

I led the flow and interface design for a multilingual iPad shipping service at La Vallée Village. Staff guide the shipping choices; shoppers enter their own parcel and contact details. I carried the design through team reviews and handoff to external front-end development.

Background

A shipping service at the end of a shopping visit.

FTL planned a parcel service near the La Vallée Village exit for international visitors sending purchases to a hotel or an overseas address. The existing desktop tool supported operations; the new iPad needed to support a face-to-face service between staff and shoppers.

Shoppers bring

The destination, purchase details and private contact information.

Staff bring

Knowledge of shipping options, service rules and the operational hand-off.

The problem

One order depended on two people with different information.

Shoppers knew where a parcel should go and what it contained. Staff knew how the shipping service worked. A long operational form would make the shopper carry too much logistics detail; a self-service flow would remove staff guidance from decisions that needed it.

The design questionHow can staff and shoppers complete an international order together without either person having to remember every shipping rule?

Process · Three design decisions

Turn shipping rules into choices people can make together.

The flow uses seven focused steps instead of one exhaustive form. The order starts with destination because it changes which services and information are relevant.

    Destination

    Set the route and available services.

  2. 02
    Weight

    Capture parcel information.

  3. 03
    Services

    Compare delivery options.

  4. 04
    Item

    Describe what is being sent.

  5. 05
    Contact

    Enter recipient information.

  6. 06
    Insurance

    Review protection for the parcel.

  7. 07
    Finish

    Return the order to staff for the next step.

01Ask for the destination first.

Destination affects carrier availability, delivery time, price, insurance and required details. Asking it first narrows the route before the shopper enters information that may not apply.

01 / Visible in the screen

Popular-country shortcuts sit above search. The first step establishes the route before parcel details.

The trade-off

Shortcuts speed up common choices, but the country list still needs validation against the actual visitor mix.

02Compare services by trade-off.

A consistent option layout puts service, speed and starting price in one view. “Lowest price” and “Fastest” help staff explain the options without making shoppers interpret a carrier list.

02 / Visible in the screen

Each option uses the same service, delivery-time and starting-price columns. Labels expose the price–speed choice.

The trade-off

A starting price makes comparison easier, but the final charge still needs explanation before payment.

03Make parcel information specific enough to use.

An open text field leaves item descriptions difficult to interpret. I separated brand, description, quantity and photos so shoppers could provide concrete parcel information while staff remained available to help.

03 / Visible in the screen

Brand, description, quantity and photos turn an ambiguous description into separate inputs.

The trade-off

More structure asks more of the shopper. Photo requirements and item-search coverage need testing in the service setting.

What the evidence supports

I delivered the end-to-end flow, core UI, high-fidelity prototype and component specifications. External front-end development completed the implementation. The service did not enter store operation, so there is no post-launch dataset from which to claim efficiency or business impact.

Reflections

What I would de-risk before a store pilot.

01 · Shared use

Test the device hand-off.

Observe staff and shoppers completing an order together. Look for hesitation when control moves between people.

Observe: hand-off prompts and requests for help.
02 · Recovery

Interrupt the flow deliberately.

Check whether people can resume, understand disabled actions and return without losing their inputs.

Observe: resumption, backtracking and input retention.
03 · Readiness

Trace the order beyond Finish.

Rehearse payment, staff ownership and carrier tracking before planning a store pilot.

Observe: whether each next step has a clear owner.

Proposed next validation, not completed research. I would also strengthen low-contrast text and progress states before putting the interface into that test.
