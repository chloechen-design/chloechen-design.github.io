02 · Context

The problem was not invoicing. It was losing momentum between tasks.

The earlier tool could store invoices, but its fragmented routes made small actions feel like separate jobs. On mobile, every context switch adds effort.

My framingI treated speed as continuity rather than fewer features: keep the user’s current object, choices and next action visible from start to finish.

  • 01Repetitive screens with limited prioritisation
  • 02Creation and management routes split across disconnected moments
  • 03Weak visual hierarchy for amounts, statuses and next actions
  • 04Incomplete CRUD, validation and feedback states
Evidence boundary

This is an independent concept design based on UX analysis and product hypotheses. No user interviews, launch metrics or business impact are claimed.

03 · Challenge

Reduce friction without hiding the information that makes an invoice trustworthy.

01

Prioritise, rather than compress.

DecisionI surfaced totals, status and the next useful action, instead of shrinking a desktop dashboard into 390 pixels. The result is a calmer first scan.

02

Preserve context across CRUD.

DecisionA missing client can be created inside the invoice flow and returns already selected. The detour solves the problem without becoming a second journey.

03

Make system feedback do real work.

DecisionSelected, loading, empty, validation and success states explain what changed and what happens next, reducing dependence on instructional copy.

04 · Architecture

Four mental models were enough to hold the whole product.

I deliberately limited the primary navigation to four spaces. Every daily action belongs to one of them, so the bottom bar stays predictable while creation remains contextual.

05 · Key flow

Invoice creation feels lightweight because the complexity arrives progressively.

I split the flow around user decisions—not database fields. Client, invoice information, line items and review each have a clear moment, while progress and preserved data maintain orientation.

06 · Wireframes

I used fidelity to answer product questions, not to decorate placeholders.

The mid-fidelity pass included realistic totals, statuses and actions because empty boxes could not reveal hierarchy problems. It let me check whether each screen answered three questions: where am I, what matters now and what can I do next?

01 · Interruption

What if the client does not exist?

Create them within the current task, validate the required fields and return to the invoice with the new client selected.

02 · Confidence

What must be checked before send?

Keep the recipient, service breakdown, tax and total visible together at the review point. Sending is a distinct commitment.

03 · Continuity

What happens after sending?

Move to an invoice detail with a clear status and timeline, so the user has a durable place to check and act later.

07 · System

The system makes status legible before it makes the interface distinctive.

I kept one deep-green accent for orientation and action. Colour never carries meaning alone: label, icon and hierarchy reinforce every invoice state.

08 · Final experience

Seven screens. One continuous job.

Select a screen to inspect the interface at reading size. Each decision links a product need to the specific part of the UI that supports it.

01 / Orientation

Home answers the question before the user asks it.

The job: understand what needs attention and start a new invoice.

The most actionable signals—outstanding money, overdue amount and recent invoices—come before secondary analytics. One creation control stays accessible without making the whole page a menu.

Design trade-off

The overview is intentionally selective. A fuller report would add density to a screen used for quick decisions.

02 / Recovery

Add the missing client without abandoning the invoice.

The job: continue creation when a prerequisite is missing.

The client form appears as a contextual sheet over the invoice. Inline errors identify which fields need correction. Completing it selects that client and returns to the original task.

Why a sheet?

It signals a temporary detour. The invoice stays conceptually present instead of becoming a separate creation journey.

03 / Composition

Build the invoice while seeing its financial consequence.

The job: add services, check tax and reach an accurate total.

A step indicator gives orientation; editable client and service rows keep corrections close to the source. The running total places price and tax beside the work that changes them, while the fixed bottom action moves to review.

Design trade-off

Guided steps reduce first-use ambiguity, but may slow repeat work. Duplication and saved line items belong in a future test.

04 / Confirmation

Make the send result unambiguous.

The job: know the invoice was sent and what to do next.

A confirmation message appears on the resulting invoice, then the detail becomes a durable record. The success state is attached to the object it changed, avoiding an isolated “done” screen.

What to validate

In a working product, sending and delivery would need separate states, including retry and failure. This prototype demonstrates the success path only.

05 / Follow-up

The detail is a place to return, not a dead end.

The job: inspect the invoice, understand its status and act on payment.

The amount and status lead; client and service details support verification. A short timeline explains what happened, while download and mark-paid actions remain at the bottom for the next task.

Why the timeline?

A status badge names the present state; a sequence gives context for how the invoice reached it.

06 / Management

Find the invoice that needs work.

The job: scan many invoices and focus on an exception.

Search supports known items; status filters support an intent such as “show overdue.” Each row repeats the same amount, client and state hierarchy. Colour reinforces text labels, so the scan does not depend on colour alone.

Design trade-off

The list favours status and amount over every field. Full information lives in the detail view.

07 / Configuration

Move infrequent setup out of the daily workflow.

The job: maintain identity, payment details and preferences.

Business and bank details are grouped by the information they control. Notification preferences sit separately; the unavailable dark-mode option is explicitly marked as coming later rather than presented as functional.

Why here?

Stable object-based navigation keeps settings reachable without giving them the same visual weight as sending and tracking invoices.

One product, distinct decisions

Overview when orienting. Context when creating. Explicit feedback when committing. A stable record when returning.

Reflection

The strongest simplification was preserving context—not removing steps.

What I learned

Fewer screens do not automatically mean less friction.

A clear sequence can feel faster than one overloaded form when progress, data and next actions remain visible.

Trade-off

The flow favours guidance over expert-speed shortcuts.

For frequent users, I would explore reusable line items, duplication and draft templates without weakening the first-use model.

Next iteration

Validate the product logic with real independent workers.

I would test terminology, interruption recovery, tax cases and the speed of repeat invoicing before extending the feature set.
