What the evidence can show

The materials document research, design decisions, prototypes, a Figma library, Storybook and team communication. They do not provide verified adoption, productivity or error-reduction metrics.

01 · Context

The warehouse depended on an internal WMS that had become difficult to maintain.

FTL already operated an internal warehouse management system. As logistics needs and workflows grew, the existing experience presented ergonomic and maintainability challenges. The brief combined two connected needs: make day-to-day work clearer for warehouse teams, and give future product changes a more consistent interface foundation.

At the product level

Improve the work people do every day.

Clarify navigation, task flows and information on WMS and PDA screens used in operational settings.

At the system level

Make repeated decisions reusable.

Build shared foundations and components so similar controls and states do not need to be redefined on every screen.

02 · Evidence

Look at the work through three lenses: people, product and delivery.

Rather than treating the design system as a visual cleanup, the project combined user input with an audit of the product and the way changes reached development.

Research input

Different roles described different parts of the same system.

Interview materials span warehouse-floor staff, managers and operations, and product or development roles. An internal WMS survey gathered usage habits, difficulties, unmet expectations and suggestions for future improvements.

Why it matteredFrontline tasks and system constraints had to be considered together.

Interface audit

Small inconsistencies accumulated into real interpretation work.

The audit called out unclear navigation structure, labels and icons that were hard to recognize, inconsistent button and tag use, and technical error messages that did not explain what to do next.

Why it matteredStandardization needed to cover meaning and state, not just colour and shape.

Ways of working

The system had to work across roles and release cycles.

Product, design, operations and international development collaborated through Agile sprints. Jira tickets, a Help Center and internal release notes helped communicate work, questions and product changes.

Why it matteredDocumentation and feedback channels were part of the system's delivery, not decoration around it.

03 · From evidence to requirements

Turn verbatims into needs, then into changes a team can act on.

The research synthesis used a VerbATIM → Need → Requirement structure: keep the user's words visible, infer the need carefully, then write a product requirement the team can discuss and test.

01 · Verbatim
“Overall, the WMS interface is intuitive if you use it frequently.”
Internal WMS survey · translated from French
02 · Need

Recognize the right action and location without relying on repeated use or memory.

03 · Requirement

Use predictable navigation, recognizable labels and consistent control states across related tasks.

This is one traceable example of the synthesis logic. It does not claim that one survey response alone set the roadmap.

Research→Audit→Prioritize→Prototype→Review
04 · Design decisions

Three decisions connected usability work to a system teams could maintain.

01

Fix the meaning of controls before polishing their appearance.

The audit exposed unclear labels, inconsistent button and tag use, and weakly explained errors. I treated component semantics, variants and feedback states as part of the UX problem, so that colour and visual polish would reinforce a rule people could understand.

02

Make shared foundations flexible enough for different operational tasks.

Buttons, fields, tags, tables, navigation and feedback recur across modules, but warehouse workflows are not identical. The library established reusable rules and variants; product screens still had to apply them to the task, role and information density in context.

03

Use product screens to make the system accountable to real work.

A component library is only useful when its rules survive a real workflow. I applied the patterns to high-fidelity WMS screens and related PDA flows, then used prototype reviews and functional checks with product and development to refine the interface.

05 · The system

Foundations, components and product patterns formed one working language.

The Figma library grouped foundations such as colour, typography and layout with reusable controls and WMS-specific patterns. Variants and states made the rules inspectable. High-fidelity WMS and PDA work showed how the library behaved in context.

06 · Adoption is part of the product

Give the team a way to find, discuss and carry the rules forward.

Figma defined the design language. Storybook gave developers an interactive place to inspect components. Jira, a Help Center and internal release notes supported questions, handoff and awareness of WMS changes.

Impact boundary

These artifacts show the work was researched, designed and connected to delivery. The material available here does not verify adoption rate, reduced implementation errors, faster task completion or productivity gains. Those outcomes would need post-release usage data and operational follow-up.

08 · Reflection

What I would measure before calling a system successful.

For the next phase, I would follow a small number of signals across the work: whether teams choose the shared components, which patterns are still duplicated, where implementation diverges from the specification, and whether warehouse staff complete priority tasks with fewer pauses or requests for help.

Adoption

Track component reuse and identify where teams work around the library.

Quality

Review implementation differences and repeated design or engineering questions.

Task experience

Test high-frequency WMS and PDA tasks with frontline staff in the operational context.

These are proposed measures for a future iteration, not results reported from this project.
